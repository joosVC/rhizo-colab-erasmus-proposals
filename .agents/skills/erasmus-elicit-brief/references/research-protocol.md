# Frame a useful scientific exploration

## Select the route

| Starting point | Research aim | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specific problem, no solution | Map mechanisms, established approaches and promising alternatives for the described need | Assuming that the literature has one best intervention |
| Preferred solution or transfer | Examine effects, limitations, alternative explanations and comparable approaches for the intended change | Treating enthusiasm or an inspiring project as validation |
| Broad need | Distinguish constructs, barriers and plausible mechanisms before comparing responses | Inventing a local cause or treating diversity itself as a deficit |

Use population/concept/context to structure a landscape question. For an effect question, include intervention, outcome and relevant comparison where known; do not manufacture a comparator or numerical effect target. Keep local relevance distinct from general scientific evidence. A narrow intervention can address a broadly recognisable need; breadth is not a popularity test.

Ask about population, situation or desired change only if unresolved and consequential. Example: “Wanneer jullie participatie zeggen, gaat het vooral om spreken in de les, meebeslissen op school, of iets anders?” Do not make a participant pick an arbitrary answer. If they do not know, ask the research to distinguish these constructs and keep the prompt exploratory.

## Explore adjacent fields deliberately

Use a core challenge plus up to three adjacent lenses. Explain their possible connection in ordinary school language. Do not list every neighbouring discipline. Choose lenses close enough to inform a feasible educational response, not merely concepts that sound novel.

- For a broad need, explore different definitions and mechanisms without selecting a cause.
- For a preferred solution, broaden to comparable mechanisms or interventions without erasing the original question.
- For a focused problem, compare approaches addressing that problem rather than replacing it with a fashionable topic.

Example lenses are candidate search directions, not evidence-backed recommendations. Let Elicit retain, reject or refine them based on the literature. Distinguish **educational intervention**, **research/co-design method**, **delivery mechanism** and **theoretical framework**: PAR, for example, can structure inquiry without identifying the intervention whose effects are being sought.

## Set search boundaries

Prioritise the supplied population and setting. If unspecified, say so; use the stated education sector without inventing an age range, school type, country restriction or support strategy. Invite sensitivity to differences within the population when relevant, not unsupported subgroup labels.

Include international research; prioritise directly relevant European contexts where available without excluding informative evidence elsewhere. Separate direct evidence from adjacent ages, higher education, youth work, clinical settings or informal education. State the adaptations and uncertainties involved in any transfer.

Seek current syntheses and recent primary studies while retaining older foundational work. Normally emphasise the preceding five years for emerging developments, anchored to the current research date, **without making that a global exclusion window**. Honour explicit date limits and state what those omit. Do not label something emerging based solely on publication year; request dated evidence of development, replication or new application.

Use systematic reviews/meta-analyses for tested effects where relevant, plus primary intervention studies, implementation studies and qualitative/mixed-methods work for experiences, mechanisms and contextual fit. Separate conceptual work and preprints from evaluated effects. Do not force every topic into an RCT-only filter or call this preparation a full systematic review.

## Request honest comparison

Ask Elicit to identify source-supported approaches, then compare:

- The challenge and intended outcomes addressed, with definitions and actual measures.
- Proposed mechanism versus mechanism investigated; intervention versus framework or design method.
- Evidence base: study designs, populations, settings, key findings, limitations and conflicting/null results; distinguish correlation from causal inference.
- Maturity and confidence with reasons. Use “established”, “emerging” or “mainly conceptual” as descriptive categories, not automatic quality ratings or formal GRADE judgments.
- Contextual relevance, prerequisites, teacher workload, equity/access barriers and implementation feasibility; separate reported conditions from suggested adaptations.
- Uptake or implementation gaps **only if studied**, with source and scope. Otherwise mark unknown. Do not infer low use from missing papers.
- Possible project direction: adapt, develop, apply or test, explicitly framed as an option for later discussion.

Prefer a readable table and deeper discussion of the most relevant directions; do not require all fields for every paper. Request traceable author/year/title/DOI or stable links and source attribution for key claims. Ask for search date, actual coverage, selection approach and limitations as far as Elicit exposes them. Do not demand invented precision, fabricated search counts or a PRISMA claim.

## Keep room for decisions

Request a small evidence-linked shortlist, usually two to five distinct directions where supported. Fewer is valid. An emerging approach may enter as a development/testing opportunity if its mechanism, relevance and uncertainty are transparent. Include weak or unsuitable directions briefly where that explains exclusion.

Ask what each direction opens up and what remains to validate locally. Request possible combinations only with an explicit mechanism-level rationale; evidence for each component is not evidence for the combined package. Leave partner, call, budget and final choice open. Literature may inform conceptual novelty or studied uptake; novelty within Erasmus+ remains unassessed until the later PRP screening.
